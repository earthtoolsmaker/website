The Wadden Sea is the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in
the world, stretching along the coasts of the Netherlands, Germany and
Denmark.{{< cite "whs" >}} It covers about 10,000 km² of tidal flats, channels and
sandbanks,{{< cite "wiki" >}} and has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since
2009.{{< cite "wiki" >}}

![Map of the Wadden Sea along the Dutch, German and Danish coasts](/images/projects/wadden_sea_seal_monitoring/wadden-sea-map.png)

## Why it matters

Some 10 to 12 million birds pass through every year, with up to 6.1 million present
at once, making it a crucial stop on the East Atlantic flyway.{{< cite "whs" >}} Its
sandbanks are haul-outs and nurseries for harbour and grey seals.{{< cite "whs" >}}

## Under pressure

Its outlook is rated "good with some concerns": climate change and sea level rise
threaten the low tidal flats, and shipping, energy development and extraction add up
around them.{{< cite "whs" >}}

## What we do

We work on this coast in three ways: [counting and recognising seals](/projects/wadden_sea_seal_monitoring/)
from aerial surveys, measuring [bird flu's toll on seabird colonies](/projects/bird_flu_monitoring/)
by drone, and [mapping how the coastline at Wierum erodes and builds up](/projects/coastal_erosion_monitoring/)
from repeated drone flights.
