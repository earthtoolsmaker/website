Pacific salmon hatch in cold North Pacific rivers, grow up at sea, and come back to
the stream they were born in to spawn.{{< cite "wiki" >}}{{< cite "salmon" >}} Chinook, Coho, Sockeye,
Pink and Chum spawn once and die; Steelhead, the sea-run form of rainbow trout, can
survive to spawn again.{{< cite "wiki" >}} These are the six our models tell apart.

{{< salmon_species >}}

## Why they matter

In the Pacific Northwest and Alaska, salmon are a keystone species.{{< cite "salmon" >}}
Each run carries the ocean upstream: spawned-out fish sink into shallow,
nutrient-poor streams and feed them as they break down, and bears hauling salmon
into the forest leave behind up to 24% of the nitrogen in riverside woodland, traced
in spruce needles 500 m from the water.{{< cite "salmon" >}}

![The Pacific salmon life cycle, from eggs in river gravel to adults returning from the ocean](/images/projects/wild_salmon_migration_monitoring/lifecycle_salmon.png)

## Under pressure

Wild runs have been hit by overfishing, habitat loss, dams, competition with
hatchery fish and a warming ocean.{{< cite "wiki" >}} Knowing how many fish actually
make it back each year is the first thing managers need, and the hardest to get by
hand.

## What we do

With [SalmonVision](/tools/salmonvision/), underwater cameras, sonar and drones feed
models that count and classify salmon as they move upstream, so river teams can
follow a run in near real time. Our [smolt sonar work](/projects/monitoring_smolt_salmon_migration_with_sonar/)
follows the young fish on their way out to sea.
